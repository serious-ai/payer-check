A working demo for medical group revenue teams

Ranking payers by denials misses what each one costs you

A health plan can cost a practice money in three ways that a denial report never ranks: it pays late, it pays short of what the contract says, and it takes staff hours to chase both. This page reads the payment files a practice already gets from every plan (the 835 remits) and prices all three, payer by payer, as one figure: what the payer costs you for every $100 it pays.

It runs on a made-up gastroenterology group with 24 clinicians and five made-up health plans, over 90 days. Pick one claim and watch it move from the visit to that number, then change the assumptions in section 3 and see whether the ranking holds.

The payer cost triangle
Paid lateDays from the payer receiving the claim to the money arriving. Priced here as the staff status checks a slow claim triggers.
Paid shortLines paid below the contract rate, often an old rate after a raise, or a visit paid at a lower code than billed.
Paid by your staffThe cost your team absorbs in hours: every extra time someone reworks, re-posts or chases a claim, at their hourly cost.

    3.The risky part, and try to break it

    The ranking leans on a contract table and a few staff-time guesses, so push on each one and watch what moves.

    Where this can go wrong

    • The contract terms. Every short-pay flag compares the payment with a rate table. If the escalator date or percent is read wrong, every flag after that date is wrong too. That is why a person confirms each contract term before any line is checked.
    • The minutes per touch. The defaults are placeholders. Until a short time log from your own team replaces them, the staff corner is an estimate.
    • The claim-received date. Days to pay start when the payer received the claim. Some payers leave that date out of the 835, so the clock would start from your own billing system instead.
    • Denied dollars. A denial is not always money lost. Counting all of it as cost would overstate the payers that deny the most, and counting none of it understates them, so the default is the national figure (46% never paid, from Premier's 2024 survey of hospitals, source 4) and the control beside this list lets you set your own.
    • One 90-day window. A quarter can run high or low, and the yearly figures carry that forward.

    4.Assumptions to confirm with your team

    Each placeholder below is a question your billing team can answer in a few minutes, and most are switchable in section 3.

    1. How many minutes does each touch take your staff?In the demo: 20 for a denied claim, 15 to review a visit paid at a lower code, 5 to post and flag a short pay, 10 to re-post a reversal, 6 for a status check. Switchable in section 3. A two-week time log on a sample of each would settle it.
    2. What does an hour of billing staff time cost you, with benefits?In the demo: $30 an hour. Switchable in section 3.
    3. What does each contract pay, and when do its rates change?In the demo: Sample Plan A at 145% of a sample Medicare rate, B at 138%, C at 140% with a 5% raise from June 1, 2026, D at 130%. The escalator date and size are switchable in section 3.
    4. When is a claim slow enough that someone checks on it?In the demo: after 30 days from the day the payer received it, one status check per claim. Switchable in section 3.
    5. How much denied money comes back after rework?In the demo: 54%, so 46% of denied dollars count as lost for good. That is the national figure from Premier's 2024 survey of hospitals and health systems, which counts claims, not dollars, so your own share may run higher or lower. Switchable in section 3.
    6. Do your payers send the claim-received date in their 835s?In the demo: every sample remit carries it. Where a payer leaves it out, the clock would start from the date your billing system sent the claim.
    7. How small a gap is rounding?In the demo: a line within 50 cents of the contract rate counts as paid in full, so a planted 30-cent difference on Sample Plan A is left alone.
    8. What about a payer with no contract on file?In the demo: Sample Plan E is named and never priced. Finding its contract, or its published fee schedule, comes first.

    5.One way to test it with your own 835s

    Ninety days of your own remits and your contract rate sheets are enough to see whether your two rankings disagree too.

    FirstGather 90 days of files

    The 835 files your clearinghouse or practice management system already keeps, plus the rate sheet for each contract. Days to pay need real dates, so this step runs under a BAA or a limited data set agreement before any file moves.

    ThenRun the same code on them

    The same plain code as this page, with your contracts confirmed term by term by someone on your team. You get this page with your own payers on it.

    ThenCompare the two rankings

    Rank by denied dollars, rank by cost per $100 paid, and set your own minutes from a short time log. Then decide which payer conversation to have first.

    MeasureMade-up sampleYour 835s

    What would prove it wrong

    • Your payers rank in the same order by denied dollars and by cost per $100 paid. Then the denial report you already have is enough.
    • Fewer than 1 in 100 lines pays below contract, and every payer's median days to pay sit within a week of each other.
    • Your own time log puts the minutes per touch far enough from the defaults that the order flips, and stays flipped across the range your team agrees on.
    • Your contracts can't be matched to the codes on the remits, which happens with some carve-outs (a service paid outside the main rate table) and case rates (one flat fee for a whole episode of care).

    How we would calibrate it on your files

    • Someone on your billing team checks 50 flagged short pays and 50 unflagged lines against the payer's own explanation of benefits, and the rule has to agree on all 100 before the sheet is used.
    • Every contract term the model reads carries the page and paragraph it came from, and nothing is checked against a term a person hasn't confirmed.
    • The minutes per touch come from a two-week time log, and the page shows the ranking at the low and high ends of what the log found.
    • The build stops when an 835 total doesn't match the sum of its lines, the same check a payment posting team runs.
    B.The pipeline, end to endPlain code does every step that touches a claim. A model reads each contract once, and drafts the note to each payer, with a person approving both.
    1. Files inPlain code835 files from the clearinghouse, weekly.
    2. Read every linePlain codeThe 835 is a fixed standard, so code reads it exactly, every time.
    3. Pair reversalsPlain codeA taken-back payment and its repayment are matched by line number.
    4. Read each contractModel passOnce per contract: rates, escalators, effective dates, each with its page cited. A person confirms.
    5. Sort every linePlain codePaid as contracted, paid short, paid at a lower code, or denied with its reason code.
    6. Count the touchesPlain codeDenials, short pays, reversals and slow claims, priced at your minutes and hourly cost.

    C.Three ways in, from least to most change for your IT teamThe first tier needs no integration at all.
    Tier 1, no integration

    Files by hand

    Someone exports 90 days of 835s from the clearinghouse portal and shares the contract rate sheets.

    Your IT team gives

    Nothing beyond a secure way to share files under the BAA.

    Timing

    About a week from the files arriving.

    Tier 2, read-only

    A scheduled drop

    The clearinghouse copies each week's 835s to a secure folder the sheet reads from, so the ranking refreshes on its own.

    Your IT team gives
    • A read-only secure file transfer (SFTP) folder, or a second delivery address at the clearinghouse.
    • No access to the practice management system.
    Timing

    Two to three weeks, mostly clearinghouse setup.

    Tier 3, in the workflow

    Flags in the work queue

    Short-pay flags and slow claims land in the billing team's own work queue, and payer notes go to the contracting lead to approve.

    Your IT team gives
    • A write path into the work queue, through the practice management system's interface or a task import.
    • Sign-in for the people who approve notes.
    Timing

    Set by your change process more than the build.
